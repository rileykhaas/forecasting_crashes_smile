\begin{abstract}
We replicate Martin \& Shi (2025), which shows that the shape of the
option-implied volatility smile carries a model-free signal for the physical
probability that a stock crashes. From index and single-name options, we recover
risk-neutral return distributions, apply a power-utility fear correction, and
form Fr\'echet--Hoeffding bounds on the crash probability; the lower bound is
predicted to approximately equal the truth. Over the paper's 1996--2022 sample we
reproduce Table~1 to within a tight tolerance: the lower bound tracks the realized
crash frequency while the risk-neutral probability and the upper bound overstate
crash risk. We then extend every exhibit through 2024 and add sector-level crash
series and a Silicon Valley Bank case study. The full analysis regenerates
end-to-end from WRDS via a single \texttt{doit} command.
\end{abstract}
\end{titlepage}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================
Martin \& Shi (2025) derive option-implied \emph{bounds} on the physical
probability that an individual stock crashes over horizons of one to twelve
months. The risk-neutral crash probability, read directly from option prices,
``cries wolf'' --- it overstates crash risk, most severely in crises, because it
bundles a fear premium. The paper's contribution is a theory-based correction:
weighting by a power-utility investor's marginal value of a dollar and closing the
unknown stock--market dependence with copula bounds yields a \emph{lower bound}
that, under mild comonotonicity, should sit close to the true crash probability.

This project reproduces that result as a fully reproducible analytical pipeline.
Starting from raw CRSP and OptionMetrics data on WRDS, we build the cleaned
firm-month panel, recover the risk-neutral densities, form the bounds, and test
them against realized crashes. We then push every exhibit past the paper's 2022
cutoff and add extensions of our own. The document that follows contains only the
resulting tables and figures and a high-level discussion; all code lives in the
repository. Exhibits that reproduce a Martin \& Shi result are numbered R1, R2,
\ldots{} (Sections~\ref{sec:replication} and~\ref{sec:extension2025}); our own
extensions are numbered E1, E2, \ldots{} (Sections~\ref{sec:eda}
and~\ref{sec:extensions}).

\subsection{Where we found success}
The core replication succeeds, and it is fully reproducible.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The central result reproduces.} Our summary-statistics table
    (Table~\ref{tab:table1}) matches the paper's cross-section to within $0.007$:
    the lower bound hugs the realized crash frequency while the risk-neutral
    probability and (more so) the upper bound overstate crashes, exactly the
    pattern the theory predicts. The calibration regressions
    (Table~\ref{tab:table2}) recover the lower bound's slope of $\beta \approx 1$
    against a miscalibrated risk-neutral probability.
  \item \textbf{One command, end to end.} The entire pipeline, WRDS pulls,
    cleaning, estimation, every figure and table, the notebook, and this report
    regenerates from a clean checkout with a single \texttt{doit} run, and every
    replicated number is pinned to the paper by a tolerance unit test (142 tests in
    all, 152 tests including the package).
  \item \textbf{The result survives out of sample.} Extending every exhibit through
    2024 (Section~\ref{sec:extension2025}) leaves the pattern intact: the
    summary statistics move by at most $0.007$ and the lower bound's forecasting
    edge holds through the COVID aftermath, the 2022 drawdown, and the March-2023
    bank stress.
  \item \textbf{The extensions work.} Pricing the eleven sector ETFs directly
    (Section~\ref{sec:extensions}) reproduces the lower-bound-tracks-realized
    finding at the sector level; the FF49 industry comparison shows why an
    average-of-constituents proxy overstates a diversified sector; and a daily
    Silicon Valley Bank case study reads one crash at three levels.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Where the challenges were}
The harder problems were practical and numerical rather than conceptual.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Numerical consistency.} The fitted risk-neutral marginals
    (isotonic-regressed CDFs) and the option-price moment integrals are two
    representations of the same distribution that can disagree at short maturities,
    which briefly broke the theoretical bound ordering; we resolved it by taking the
    boundary derivative in Result~5 from the fitted marginal, exactly as Appendix~D
    prescribes.
  \item \textbf{Scale.} Forming a risk-neutral density for every name, month, and
    maturity across a 35-million-row surface exhausted memory until the pipeline was
    restructured to stream one day at a time.
  \item \textbf{Data gaps.} OptionMetrics is missing the July~2020 surface snapshot
    entirely, which we handle by falling back to that month's last valid trading day.
  \item \textbf{Membership freeze.} The S\&P~500 \emph{constituent} list in our CRSP
    vintage ends December~2024, while the OptionMetrics surface runs to August~2025.
    Rather than drop the most recent months, we carry the last known membership
    forward (the documented \texttt{carried\_forward} freeze), which keeps the
    single-name surfaces populated to the option data's last date and affects only
    the eight 2025 formation months (January--August~2025).
  \item \textbf{Extension frontier.} The \emph{bounds} themselves nonetheless stop at
    year-end~2024, not August~2025: every constituent bound is priced against the
    market (SPX) CDF, whose spot is the CRSP S\&P~500 index level (\texttt{spindx}),
    and that too ends December~2024. We keep CRSP rather than push further for
    \emph{data consistency} with the paper --- moneyness ($K/S$) and the
    realized-crash outcome are defined on a single CRSP source, so the estimator
    never mixes datasets across the sample. Reaching the surface frontier would mean
    sourcing the index spot from OptionMetrics (\texttt{secprd}), as our SVB study
    already does; we flag it as documented future work over a calm eight-month
    window (January--August~2025) that changes no conclusion.
  \item \textbf{Universe differences.} Our CRSP membership vintage yields a modestly
    larger firm set than the paper's, a difference we document rather than force.
  \item \textbf{Classification fidelity.} Matching the paper's industry analysis
    required the full Fama--French 49-industry scheme (its Figure~10), and rolling
    those industries up to the eleven tradable sector ETFs is only unambiguous for
    five of them, a mapping we make explicit rather than paper over
    (Section~\ref{sec:extensions}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Data}
%======================================================================
All raw data comes from WRDS and regenerates from scratch via \texttt{doit}; the
proprietary pulls cache locally and are never committed. The sample follows the
paper: S\&P~500 index members observed on the last trading day of each month,
January 1996 through December 2022, and our extension carries the panel to the
most recent available data.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{OptionMetrics IvyDB} --- the standardized volatility surface
    (\texttt{vsurfd}) is the core input (the ``smile''); the zero-coupon curve
    (\texttt{zerocd}) supplies risk-free discounting; the security-name history
    (\texttt{secnmd}) resolves \texttt{secid} labels.
  \item \textbf{CRSP} --- the monthly stock file (\texttt{msf\_v2}, CIZ~2.0)
    provides realized returns and crash events with delisting returns folded into
    \texttt{mthret} (so wipeouts such as SVB register as crashes); the S\&P~500
    constituent list (\texttt{msp500list}) drives sample selection; the index file
    (\texttt{msix}) supplies the S\&P~500 level as the index spot.
  \item \textbf{WRDS CRSP--OptionMetrics link} joins option data (\texttt{secid})
    to CRSP returns (\texttt{permno}).
\end{itemize}

The extensions draw on the same two providers. The eleven Select Sector SPDR ETFs
(plus the regional-bank ETF KRE) are pulled from the identical OptionMetrics surface
and priced by the unchanged engine; they are force-matched into CRSP for their spot
and realized returns and never enter the replication cross-section. The Silicon
Valley Bank case study uses a small, self-contained \emph{daily} OptionMetrics pull
(surface, spot, and zero curve) for four securities over February--March~2023.

Financial firms are deliberately retained --- the universe is pure S\&P~500
membership with no industry screen, which is central to the SVB extension. All raw
pulls are proprietary and cache locally; none are committed to the repository.

\section{Methodology}
%======================================================================
For each firm and month we turn the option-implied smile into a crash-probability
estimate through five steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Risk-neutral density.} Price out-of-the-money options on a fine
    moneyness grid, differentiate (Breeden--Litzenberger) to recover the
    risk-neutral CDF of the gross return, and enforce a valid distribution with an
    isotonic fit.
  \item \emph{Risk-neutral crash probability.} Read $P^{*}[R \le q]$ directly from
    that CDF.
  \item \emph{Fear correction.} Reweight by a power-utility investor's marginal
    utility ($\gamma = 2$, calibrated by the paper on the market) to move toward
    the physical measure.
  \item \emph{Copula bounds.} Close the unknown stock--market dependence with
    Fr\'echet--Hoeffding bounds; the comonotone \emph{lower bound} is the
    forecaster.
  \item \emph{Calibration test.} Regress the realized crash indicator on each
    probability. The theory predicts a slope of one for the lower bound.
\end{enumerate}

We follow the paper's Appendix~D throughout: a fine grid $K/S \in [1/L, L]$
($L = 3$ at one to six months, $5$ at twelve), implied volatility interpolated
linearly within observed strikes and held flat outside, zero-dividend
Black--Scholes prices, and moments computed from option prices by the midpoint
rule. Table schemas are fixed centrally and every intermediate table is a tidy,
documented dataframe.

The extensions reuse this same five-step engine without modification. The sector
ETFs are priced exactly like any name, so their bounds are directly comparable to
the single-name results. For the industry comparison we assign each S\&P~500
constituent to a Fama--French 49 industry from its CRSP SIC code, the
classification the paper uses for its Figure~10, and roll those industries up to
the sector ETFs they compose; only five of the eleven sectors map unambiguously, a
caveat we carry through the exhibit. The SVB case study runs the identical engine at
daily frequency over a fixed two-month window. Nothing about the estimator changes
across extensions; only the input panel does.

for all eleven sectors, at the paper's one-year 20\% horizon.
\end{leadin}

\subsection{How tight is each measure?}
\begin{leadin}
The pattern is consistent: the proxy (orange) sits \emph{above} the direct bound
(blue) in every sector and at almost every date. Table~\ref{tab:industry} measures each
against the sector's realized crash frequency. The proxy overstates it in every sector
but one (the Proxy$-$Real.\ column is positive throughout, by up to nine or ten
points). The direct measure is far better calibrated for the broad, well-diversified
sectors, essentially on top of realized for financials ($+0.007$) and industrials
($+0.000$), and starkest in health care and real estate, where individually risky
biotech names and single REITs keep the proxy above $0.11$ against a direct bound less
than half that. For energy, technology, and utilities the direct bound sits
\emph{below} the realized frequency: each has its own crash trigger (oil, the tech
cycle, rates) that fires independently of a broad market crash, so the comonotonicity
that makes the bound tight is weakest there and the bound stays conservative exactly as
a \emph{lower} bound should. The lone exception is communication services, whose XLC realized column
is inflated by a short, crash-heavy post-2018 sample (the ETF launched in 2018, as did
XLRE); read those two realized figures with that caveat. In every case, though, the
direct measure is the sector's own crash probability; the proxy is not.
\end{leadin}

\subsection{Silicon Valley Bank: one event at three levels}
\begin{leadin}
As a final stress test, we run the same engine at \emph{daily} frequency through the
March~2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, reading one event at three levels, the
failed name (SIVB), the regional-bank ETF (KRE), and broad financials (XLF). This is
the bound at its structural weak point: theory says it is conservative for an
\emph{idiosyncratic} crash, where the risk-neutral probability is the better estimate.
SVB is exactly that, with a known resolution --- the bank failed, the index did not.
\end{leadin}

\subsection{Checking the calls against the outcome}
\begin{leadin}
Because this event has a known resolution, we can check the probabilities against what
actually happened. Table~\ref{tab:svb} pairs each asset's peak crash probability with
its realized drawdown from the pre-collapse close (8~March) to the March trough. The
measure classifies all three levels correctly: the peak probabilities
($36\% \to 18\% \to 6\%$) rank-order the realized drawdowns
($-60\% \to -27\% \to -11\%$), and the $20\%$ threshold cleanly separates the two that
crashed (SIVB, delisted; KRE, $-27\%$) from the one that did not (XLF, $-11\%$). A
single event is an anecdote, not a calibration, but it is the anecdote the theory
predicts.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
%======================================================================
The replication confirms the paper's central claim on our independently
constructed panel: the option-implied lower bound is a well-calibrated forecast of
crash risk, materially tighter than the risk-neutral probability it corrects. The
main frictions were engineering and data-quality issues, each of which we resolved 
or documented explicitly. The low regression $R^2$ (a few percent) is expected: crashes 
are rare binary events, and the meaningful comparison is the lower bound against the 
risk-neutral probability on the same sample, where the bound wins.

Our sector-ETF extension carries the method beyond the paper and sharpens its
economic point. Pricing the eleven Select Sector SPDR surfaces (plus KRE) directly
gives an honest per-sector crash gauge that spikes exactly when it should:
technology in 2000--02, financials and regional banks in 2008--09, energy in
2014--16, and confirms the ``crying wolf'' result at the sector level, with the
risk-neutral probability running two- to three-fold above realized while the lower
bound stays close. The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:industry} is the clearest payoff:
the paper's average-of-constituents proxy overstates the realized sector crash
frequency in \emph{every} sector but one (by up to nine percentage points), because an
average of individually risky names is not the probability the diversified sector
crashes. Our direct measure supplies that probability, landing on top of the
realized frequency for the broad diversified sectors (financials $0.079$ vs.\ $0.073$;
industrials $0.067$ vs.\ $0.066$) and staying conservative, as a lower bound should,
for the sectors whose crashes are sector-specific (oil, the tech cycle, rates) rather
than market-driven.
